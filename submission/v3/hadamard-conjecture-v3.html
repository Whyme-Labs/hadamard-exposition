A question about perfect balance

Imagine changing coordinates so that every original coordinate contributes equally in magnitude to every new coordinate. We also want the change to preserve lengths and angles. For real coordinates, these requirements force a remarkable object: a Hadamard matrix after normalization.

The Hadamard conjecture asks whether such perfect balance is possible in every dimension divisible by four. It does not ask whether orthogonal transformations exist. They exist in every dimension. It asks whether one can choose a particularly restrictive kind whose entries all have the same magnitude. The question has been open for more than ninety years. Every order below 668 is settled, but no method is known that covers all orders. It is listed as JSP-000013 by the Justin Sun Prize. [1]

This article has three parts. The first explains what a Hadamard matrix is, why its order must be one, two or a multiple of four, and what exactly it optimizes. The second covers the existing constructions, how far they reach, where the first unknown orders lie, and what is proved about the problem as a whole. The third describes an application in numerical rounding. It shows that the perfect balance the conjecture promises does not guarantee less rounding error. Readers interested only in the conjecture can stop after the second part.

Part I. The object

Why multiples of four appear

An order-nn Hadamard matrix is a real matrix HH with entries in {−1,+1}\{-1,+1\} satisfying

HH𝖳=nIn.HH^{\mathsf T}=nI_n.

Each row has squared length nn, and distinct rows have dot product zero. Consequently Q=H/nQ=H/\sqrt n is orthogonal, so H𝖳H=nInH^{\mathsf T}H=nI_n as well and the columns are orthogonal too. The normalization matters: HH itself multiplies lengths by n\sqrt n.

Two operations preserve the defining identity: permuting rows or columns, and multiplying a row or column by −1-1. Matrices related by these operations are called equivalent. Every Hadamard matrix is equivalent to a normalized one, whose first row and first column contain only +1+1.

Orders one and two have immediate examples, (1)(1) and (111−1)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right). For n>2n>2, orthogonality forces nn to be divisible by four. Here is the complete counting argument.

Flip whole columns until the first row consists entirely of +1+1. Column flips preserve row dot products. The second row must then contain equally many +1+1 and −1-1 entries, so nn is even. Reorder the columns so the second row has its positive entries in the first half.

Let the third row have aa positive entries in the first half and bb in the second. Orthogonality with the first row gives a+b=n/2a+b=n/2. Orthogonality with the second gives a=ba=b. Therefore a=b=n/4a=b=n/4 must be an integer.

This is a necessary condition, not a construction. The Hadamard conjecture asserts the converse: for every positive integer kk, there is a Hadamard matrix of order 4k4k. Confusing these directions would turn a short counting proof into a false solution.

An exact optimization meaning

For a real orthogonal matrix QQ, define its largest entry magnitude

μ(Q)=maxi,j|Qij|.\mu(Q)=\max_{i,j}|Q_{ij}|.

Every row has squared length one. If all entries were smaller in magnitude than 1/n1/\sqrt n, the sum of their squares would be smaller than one. Thus

μ(Q)≥1n.\mu(Q)\geq\frac1{\sqrt n}.

Equality forces every entry of every row to have magnitude exactly 1/n1/\sqrt n. Otherwise a row containing a smaller entry could not reach squared length one without another entry exceeding the bound. Hence nQ\sqrt n\,Q is a Hadamard matrix. Conversely, H/nH/\sqrt n attains equality for every Hadamard matrix HH.

This is more than a statement about individual entries. Treat xx as a row vector. For ∥x∥1≤1\|x\|_1\leq1,

|(xQ)j|≤∑i|xi||Qij|≤μ(Q).|(xQ)_j|\leq\sum_i|x_i|\,|Q_{ij}|\leq\mu(Q).

Choose xx to be a coordinate vector corresponding to an entry attaining the maximum, and equality is possible. Therefore

sup∥x∥1≤1∥xQ∥∞=μ(Q).\sup_{\|x\|_1\leq1}\|xQ\|_\infty=\mu(Q).

The worst-case peak is therefore always at least 1/n1/\sqrt n, and this lower bound is attained exactly when an order-nn Hadamard matrix exists. A minimum over all orthogonal matrices always exists, because the real orthogonal group is compact and the largest-entry function is continuous. When no Hadamard matrix of order nn exists, that minimum is strictly larger than 1/n1/\sqrt n.

The Hadamard conjecture is thus an exact optimality question: does this lower bound become attainable in every dimension n=4kn=4k? The connection with flat real orthogonal matrices is established mathematics. Jaming and Matolcsi study this viewpoint and relaxations of perfect flatness. [2]

The input constraint is essential. Replace the ℓ1\ell_1 unit ball by the ℓ2\ell_2 unit sphere and every orthogonal matrix has the same worst-case peak:

sup∥x∥2=1∥xQ∥∞=1.\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|xQ\|_\infty=1.

The upper bound follows from length preservation. The vector x=ejQ𝖳x=e_jQ^{\mathsf T} attains it. No fixed orthogonal transformation makes every unit vector less spiky.

The same object maximizes a determinant

Hadamard’s own interest was a determinant problem. [3] For any real n×nn\times n matrix AA with entries of magnitude at most one, let G=AA𝖳G=AA^{\mathsf T}. Its nonnegative eigenvalues have sum trG≤n2\operatorname{tr}G\leq n^2. Applying the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives

detG≤(n2n)n=nn,\det G\leq\left(\frac{n^2}{n}\right)^n=n^n,

and hence |detA|≤nn/2|\det A|\leq n^{n/2}. For a sign matrix, equality requires all eigenvalues of GG to equal nn. Since GG is real symmetric, this is equivalent to G=nIG=nI.

So a Hadamard matrix simultaneously saturates a determinant bound and, after normalization, the smallest possible largest-entry bound. These are two precise meanings of perfect balance. When nn is not a multiple of four, the largest determinant of a sign matrix is a separate and harder maximal determinant problem. It is open in general. [4]

Equivalent combinatorial forms

The conjecture has several equivalent forms, which is one reason it appears in so many fields. [4,5]

  • Designs. Normalize an order-4t4t Hadamard matrix and delete its first row and column. Record each remaining +1+1 as an incidence. The result is a symmetric 22-(4t−1,2t−1,t−1)(4t-1,\,2t-1,\,t-1) design, and every such design arises in this way. The conjecture is equivalent to the existence of these Hadamard designs for every tt.
  • Codes. The rows of HH and −H-H, written over {0,1}\{0,1\}, form a binary code of length nn with 2n2n codewords and minimum distance n/2n/2. Plotkin’s bound shows this is the largest possible such code, so Hadamard matrices give optimal codes.
  • Orthogonal arrays and experimental design. Hadamard matrices give two-level orthogonal arrays of strength two. In statistics these are the Plackett-Burman designs for screening many factors with few runs.

These are reformulations, not routes around the difficulty. Being equivalent, none is easier than the original.

Part II. What is known

Building matrices: products and doubling

The simplest construction combines two smaller matrices. If HmH_m and HnH_n are Hadamard, their Kronecker product Hm⊗HnH_m\otimes H_n is Hadamard of order mnmn, because

(Hm⊗Hn)(Hm⊗Hn)𝖳=HmHm𝖳⊗HnHn𝖳=mnImn.(H_m\otimes H_n)(H_m\otimes H_n)^{\mathsf T}=H_mH_m^{\mathsf T}\otimes H_nH_n^{\mathsf T}=mn\,I_{mn}.

Taking H2H_2 as one factor gives Sylvester’s 1867 doubling: [6]

H2n=(HnHnHn−Hn).H_{2n}=\begin{pmatrix}H_n&H_n\\ H_n&-H_n\end{pmatrix}.

Starting at order one gives every power of two. It does not give order twelve. Infinite is not the same as exhaustive. Products alone also never produce an order whose odd part is a new prime, so new building blocks are needed. Hadamard himself exhibited matrices of orders 12 and 20 in 1893. [3] In 1898 Scarpis showed that a Hadamard matrix of order p+1p+1, for a prime p≡3(mod4)p\equiv3\pmod4, yields one of order p(p+1)p(p+1). [4]

Paley’s construction and order twelve

Paley’s 1933 paper supplied the first systematic infinite families of new orders, from finite fields. [7] The conjecture is usually traced to that paper. We prove the prime-field case of his first construction.

Take a prime q≡3(mod4)q\equiv3\pmod4. Define the quadratic character χ\chi on residues modulo qq: it is zero at zero, +1+1 at nonzero squares, and −1-1 at non-squares. Let

Cij=χ(j−i),0≤i,j<q.C_{ij}=\chi(j-i),\qquad 0\leq i,j<q.

The nonzero squares form a subgroup of index two, so χ\chi is multiplicative and exactly half the nonzero residues have each sign. Every row sum of CC is therefore zero. Also −1-1 is not a square: a solution of t2=−1t^2=-1 would give tq−1=−1t^{q-1}=-1 when q≡3(mod4)q\equiv3\pmod4, contrary to Fermat’s little theorem. Thus χ(−1)=−1\chi(-1)=-1, giving C𝖳=−CC^{\mathsf T}=-C.

The needed correlation identity is

∑xχ(x−a)χ(x−b)={q−1,a=b,−1,a≠b.\sum_x\chi(x-a)\chi(x-b)=\begin{cases}q-1,&a=b,\\-1,&a\ne b.\end{cases}

For distinct a,ba,b, set u=(2x−a−b)/(b−a)u=(2x-a-b)/(b-a). Then (x−a)(x−b)=((b−a)/2)2(u2−1)(x-a)(x-b)=((b-a)/2)^2(u^2-1). The nonzero square factor has character one, so this invertible change of variable reduces the sum to ∑uχ(u2−1)\sum_u\chi(u^2-1). Count pairs satisfying v2=u2−1v^2=u^2-1. For each uu there are 1+χ(u2−1)1+\chi(u^2-1) choices of vv. On the other hand, (u−v)(u+v)=1(u-v)(u+v)=1. Each nonzero residue t=u−vt=u-v uniquely determines u+v=t−1u+v=t^{-1}, then uu and vv because two is invertible. There are q−1q-1 pairs. Thus q+∑uχ(u2−1)=q−1q+\sum_u\chi(u^2-1)=q-1, proving the identity.

It follows that CC𝖳=qI−JCC^{\mathsf T}=qI-J, where JJ is the all-ones matrix. Now set

H=(1𝟏𝖳𝟏C−I).H=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\\ \mathbf1&C-I\end{pmatrix}.

Every entry is a sign. The first row is orthogonal to every later row because C−IC-I has row sum −1-1. The lower-right block of HH𝖳HH^{\mathsf T} is

J+(C−I)(C−I)𝖳=J+(qI−J)+I=(q+1)I.J+(C-I)(C-I)^{\mathsf T}=J+(qI-J)+I=(q+1)I.

Therefore HH𝖳=(q+1)IHH^{\mathsf T}=(q+1)I. The same argument works over any finite field of order q≡3(mod4)q\equiv3\pmod 4, so Paley’s first construction gives order q+1q+1 for every such prime power. His second construction gives order 2(q+1)2(q+1) for every prime power q≡1(mod4)q\equiv1\pmod4. [4,7]

How far the classical methods reach

Combine Sylvester doubling, both Paley constructions over prime powers, and Kronecker products. The accompanying script known_orders.py computes exactly which orders these classical methods reach. Up to 200 they give 44 of the 50 multiples of four. The six missing orders are

92,116,156,172,184,188.92,\ 116,\ 156,\ 172,\ 184,\ 188.

These are exactly the orders below 200 that Paley himself listed as unsettled. Order 92 is the first. Paley’s first construction would need q=91=7⋅13q=91=7\cdot13, and his second would need q=45=32⋅5q=45=3^2\cdot5. Neither is a prime power. The only factorizations of 92 into two orders greater than one use 2 and 46, or 4 and 23, and neither 46 nor 23 is a Hadamard order. Williamson settled 172 in 1944, order 92 fell in 1962, and 184=2⋅92184=2\cdot92 then followed by doubling. [8,9]

Up to 1000, these three methods alone reach 195 of the 250 multiples of four. Product variants, such as Agaian’s theorem giving order 8mn8mn from orders 4m4m and 4n4n, reach a few more. [4] A missed order is not an unknown order. The classical methods miss many orders below 668, and every one of those has since been reached by other methods.

Williamson’s method and the computer

Williamson’s 1944 construction builds an order-4m4m matrix from four smaller pieces. [8] Let A,B,C,DA,B,C,D be symmetric circulant m×mm\times m sign matrices satisfying

A2+B2+C2+D2=4mIm.A^2+B^2+C^2+D^2=4m\,I_m.

Then the block matrix

H=(ABCD−BA−DC−CDA−B−D−CBA)H=\begin{pmatrix}A&B&C&D\\-B&A&-D&C\\-C&D&A&-B\\-D&-C&B&A\end{pmatrix}

is Hadamard of order 4m4m. Circulant matrices always commute with each other, and symmetry makes each block equal to its transpose. The pattern of signs is the multiplication table of the quaternions, which is why the cross terms cancel. For m=3m=3, take A=JA=J and B=C=D=2I−JB=C=D=2I-J. Then A2+3B2=3J+3(4I−4J+3J)=12IA^2+3B^2=3J+3(4I-4J+3J)=12I, giving another order-twelve matrix. The accompanying test checks this block identity directly.

The method turns a search over (4m)2(4m)^2 signs into a search over four circulant first rows of length mm, with symmetry cutting that further. In 1962 Baumert, Golomb and Hall used an early computer at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory to find Williamson matrices with m=23m=23. This gave the first Hadamard matrix of order 92. [9] It is an early example of a computer settling a case of a classical existence question.

It was once conjectured that Williamson matrices exist for every odd mm. That Williamson conjecture is false. Exhaustive searches show that Williamson matrices do not exist for m=35m=35, nor for m=47,53,59m=47,53,59. [10] Hadamard matrices of those orders exist by other means. Turyn found an infinite family of Williamson matrices. [12] Goethals and Seidel removed the symmetry requirement by inserting a reversal matrix into a modified array. [11] Baumert-Hall arrays are larger templates of the same kind. Filled with T-sequences, which are built from base sequences or Turyn-type sequences, they reach many more orders. This is how order 428 was found. Later work added difference families, further infinite families, and product theorems that build new orders from old ones. [4] Each method reaches many new orders, and none is known to reach them all.

The computational frontier

Progress at individual orders now comes from large structured searches. Kharaghani and Tayfeh-Rezaie found order 428 in 2004 by a cluster search for Turyn-type sequences, and published it in 2005. [13] Đoković constructed order 764 in 2008, using a Goethals-Seidel array. [14] According to Cati and Pasechnik’s database of constructions, consistent with the list in Đoković’s 2008 paper, the only multiples of four up to 1208 with no known Hadamard matrix are [14,15]

668,716,892,1132.668,\ 716,\ 892,\ 1132.

The smallest open case, 668=4⋅167668=4\cdot167, has resisted every method so far. Paley’s constructions would need q=667=23⋅29q=667=23\cdot29 or q=333=32⋅37q=333=3^2\cdot37, and neither is a prime power. Products do not help either: the only factorizations of 668 into two orders greater than one use 2 and 334, or 4 and 167, and neither 334 nor 167 is an admissible order. Searches with the array methods have not found the required sequences. This is evidence of difficulty, not of nonexistence. No order divisible by four has ever been shown to have no Hadamard matrix.

The number of matrices grows quickly once they exist. Up to equivalence there is one Hadamard matrix of each order 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12. Order 16 has five, order 20 has three, order 24 has 60 and order 28 has 487. [16] Order 32 has exactly 13,710,027, a classification Kharaghani and Tayfeh-Rezaie completed in 2013. [17] Abundance at the orders we can search does not translate into a general existence proof.

What is proved about all orders

The strongest general results show that every odd number appears in some Hadamard order, provided we allow a large enough power of two. In 1976 Seberry proved that for every odd qq there is a Hadamard matrix of order 2tq2^tq for all sufficiently large tt. [18] Her paper stated the explicit threshold t≈2log2(q−3)t\approx2\log_2(q-3). A 2007 note by Jensen showed that this threshold does not follow from the proof when qq has several prime factors, although the existence result stands. [18] Craigen introduced signed groups, which produce no new small orders but sharpen this asymptotic estimate. They lowered the coefficient of log2q\log_2 q to about 2/32/3. [19] Craigen and Tiessen reduced it to 3/83/8 in unpublished work, and Livinskyi to 1/51/5. [20] A 2024 preprint claims 3/203/20; it had not been published at the time of writing. [20] The conjecture asks for t=2t=2 for every odd qq, and all of these bounds still grow with qq.

Density results measure the same gap from another direction. If the conjecture holds, the number of Hadamard orders up to xx is about x/4x/4. Paley’s constructions alone give about x/logxx/\log x orders. Counting Kronecker products of Paley orders, de Launey and Gordon proved the slightly larger lower bound xe(C+o(1))(logloglogx)2/logxx\,e^{(C+o(1))(\log\log\log x)^2}/\log x, with C≈0.8178C\approx0.8178. They also showed that the other major constructions do not improve this bound. It is not even known that the Hadamard orders have positive density. [21] De Launey proved that for every ε>0\varepsilon>0, a positive proportion of odd numbers kk admit a Hadamard matrix of order k⋅22+⌊εlog2k⌋k\cdot2^{2+\lfloor\varepsilon\log_2k\rfloor}. [22] Allowing only a slowly growing power of two thus covers a positive fraction of odd parts, but not all of them.

Two related problems are also open and are sometimes confused with this one. The skew-Hadamard conjecture asks for a Hadamard matrix with H+H𝖳=2IH+H^{\mathsf T}=2I in every order divisible by four. It is stronger than the Hadamard conjecture and has its own frontier: Cati and Pasechnik list 41 orders up to 1208 with no known skew-Hadamard matrix. [15] Ryser’s circulant Hadamard conjecture asserts the opposite kind of statement: no circulant Hadamard matrix exists beyond order four. Excluding circulant matrices does not construct general Hadamard matrices. [1,4]

Why the problem is hard

Three features explain why no single idea has closed the gap.

  1. Constructions need structure. Every infinite family comes from algebra: finite fields, group rings, or sequences with special autocorrelation. Each structure exists only at orders with matching arithmetic, such as q+1q+1 for a prime power qq. The open orders are exactly those where no known structure fits.
  2. Unstructured search is hopeless at these sizes. An order-668 sign matrix has 6682=446,224668^2=446{,}224 entries. Williamson-type arrays reduce this to a few sequences of length 167. Even that space is too large to search exhaustively without further constraints that might exclude every solution.
  3. Naive probability gives the wrong answer. For a random sign matrix, two given rows are orthogonal with probability about 2/(πn)\sqrt{2/(\pi n)}. If the (n2)\binom n2 pairs were independent, the expected number of Hadamard matrices would tend to zero as nn grows. Yet order 32 alone has more than thirteen million inequivalent examples. The orthogonality events are strongly dependent, so this naive reasoning cannot settle existence either way. Rigorous probabilistic methods can count matrices with a small number of mutually orthogonal rows. [23] They are nowhere near the full square case.

The conjecture is therefore not about whether balance is plausible. It asks whether a rigid algebraic coincidence, which we can produce in many families, occurs in every admissible order.

Part III. An application: balance is not rounding

The rotation trick

Large numerical models are often stored with low-precision weights and activations. A single large coordinate forces a coarse quantization grid on everything that shares its scale. A common remedy rotates the coordinates first. For a linear layer Y=XWY=XW and any orthogonal QQ,

Xr=XQ,Wr=Q𝖳W,XrWr=XW.X_r=XQ,\qquad W_r=Q^{\mathsf T}W,\qquad X_rW_r=XW.

A scaled Hadamard matrix H/nH/\sqrt n is the natural choice. By Part I it minimizes the worst-case peak over the ℓ1\ell_1 ball, and a Sylvester matrix also has a fast transform. Methods such as QuaRot and QuIP# build randomized Hadamard rotations into language-model quantization. SpinQuant shows that the choice of rotation matters. [24,25,26] This article does not propose or evaluate such a method.

The question here is narrower. Suppose the conjecture were true, so perfectly balanced rotations existed in every admissible dimension. Would they guarantee lower rounding error? They would not. This holds even with the optimal peak, and even for every Hadamard matrix at once.

A fixed input that every Hadamard matrix hurts

Use a symmetric max-absolute-value quantizer. At bit width bb, let K=2b−1−1K=2^{b-1}-1. For a nonzero vector zz, its scale is Δ=∥z∥∞/K\Delta=\|z\|_\infty/K, and its reconstructed coordinates are ẑi=Δround(zi/Δ)\widehat z_i=\Delta\,\operatorname{round}(z_i/\Delta), with nearest rounding, ties to even, and signed codes −K,…,K-K,\ldots,K. A smaller peak gives a finer grid, but the distance from the data to the grid also matters.

As the dimension grows, the peak ratio (K+2)/(Kn)(K+2)/(K\sqrt n) tends to zero while the error stays fixed. Choosing a different Hadamard matrix, or randomizing its signs and permutations, cannot remove the error for this input, because those operations produce another Hadamard matrix. This does not contradict probabilistic guarantees for random inputs. It shows that perfect balance alone does not guarantee a better result. The theorem assumes one scale per vector. It says nothing about smaller groups, learned quantizers or real models. No first-discovery claim is made for this derivation.

The accompanying checker evaluates 1,280 rational cases across seven Paley orders and one Sylvester order, including the non-power-of-two orders 12, 20, 24 and 44. A further 672 cases cover Sylvester matrices with every supported sign pattern. Finite cases corroborate the proof; they do not replace it.

Test the counterexample family

A fresh-seed study with competing transforms

A constructed example shows what can happen; it does not show what usually happens. A separate study compares four transforms on synthetic data. Its protocol fixed seeds 1000 through 1099, every setting and one primary comparison before any of those samples were generated. [27]

Each seed creates a 512×128512\times128 Gaussian activation matrix and a 128×64128\times64 Gaussian weight matrix. Activation column zero is multiplied by 1,2,4,8,161,2,4,8,16 or 3232 to create an outlier. Four arms are compared: the original basis, a randomized Hadamard rotation, a Haar-random orthogonal matrix, and a signed permutation that reorders without mixing. Both operands receive the same four-bit quantizer, grouped over 32 input features in the primary setting. Error is the relative squared Frobenius error of the output, simulated in float64.

At the primary setting, with multiplier 32, mean error fell from 2.606175% to 1.355898% under Hadamard, a 47.97% reduction. All 100 paired seeds favored Hadamard, and the 95% whole-seed bootstrap interval for the change was [−1.307,−1.194][-1.307,\,-1.194] percentage points.

  • Generic rotations also help. A Haar-random orthogonal matrix also removes most of the outlier penalty, so the benefit is not exclusive to Hadamard.
  • No outlier, no benefit. With no outlier, Hadamard was marginally worse. For isotropic Gaussian inputs every orthogonal rotation leaves the error distribution unchanged, so this small difference is sampling noise, not a systematic effect.
  • Grid-aligned data is hurt. When the data already sit on the quantization grid, the original basis has zero error. Hadamard averaged 2.742108% error and lost on all 100 seeds. This is the mechanism of the theorem above, seen in random data.

The study retains all 7,600 measurements, input and transform hashes, and an independent audit that recomputes every reported summary from the raw data. It measures no model quality, memory or speed.

What the evidence supports

The Hadamard conjecture asks whether a perfectly balanced orthogonal matrix exists in every dimension divisible by four. Part I showed that this balance is exactly the minimum possible largest entry and the maximum possible determinant. Part II showed how far algebraic and computer-assisted constructions have reached: every order below 668, and every odd factor once enough powers of two are allowed. The general case remains open. Part III showed that the promised balance does not guarantee less rounding error. Every Hadamard matrix fails on the same fixed input, while a synthetic study shows the benefit in the case the method targets.

The constructions, equivalences and frontier results in Parts I and II are established mathematics, credited to their sources. Proofs are given where they are short enough to include. The counterexample theorem and the study are offered as teaching material, not as new quantization methods. Nothing here proves the conjecture at any new order.

Reproduce and inspect

The checks need Python and NumPy, with no GPU, model download or external dataset. Run them from the repository root.

python submission/v3/known_orders.py --limit 200
python -m unittest discover -s submission/v3 -p 'test_*.py' -v
cd submission/v2
python -m unittest discover -s strengthening -p 'test_*.py' -v
python strengthening/audit_study.py run-v2
python strengthening/all_hadamard_checks.py > new-all-hadamard-checks.json

The first two commands check the classical-order counts and the Williamson examples in Part II. The remaining commands check the Paley construction, the counterexample theorem and the retained study data. Those are unchanged from version 2 and stored in submission/v2. A fresh study run uses python strengthening/study.py --output new-run from that directory.
